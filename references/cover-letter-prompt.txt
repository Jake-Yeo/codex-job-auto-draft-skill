Write a professional cover letter in the applicant's own voice. Explain why
they would be excited to join this employer and team, what they would like
to learn from the people doing the work, and how they could contribute.
Show how that interest developed through something the applicant actually
did. End by explicitly welcoming or asking for an interview.

Find the applicant's reason to join

- Read the posting, the applicant's confirmed experience and projects, and
  their instructions for the letter. Find a verifiable detail about the
  team's product, users, or way of working that matters to this applicant.
  Explain why they would choose to do this work here. Ground that reason
  in the applicant's interests and preferred examples when available.
- Look for a change in what they understand or can do. A tool improved, a
  project exposed a problem they wanted to solve, or work with users made a
  particular result matter. Use the change that actually happened. When
  there is no stated passion, develop a credible learning interest from
  the work they chose to do, and express it as something to explore.
- Take direct feedback on a previous letter as guidance. Preserve the
  parts the applicant liked and change the parts they pointed out.

Connect past work to something they would like to learn

- Open with a concrete part of the employer's work or an experience that
  explains the applicant's interest. Introduce the role and the specific
  reason to join the team early. Give each opening sentence a distinct
  purpose.
- Develop one or two real experiences. Explain what the applicant tried,
  decided, built, or found out. Give a personal project room when it shows
  what they chose to learn or build on their own. Use a verified result
  to substantiate the story and credit teammates accurately. Make it clear
  what the experience made them curious to understand next.
- Name a skill the applicant would like to develop by working alongside
  the team's engineers or other specialists. For engineering roles, show
  a specific practice they want to learn, such as setting boundaries
  between services, designing for failures, or reproducing a failure in
  a test. Use the product and its users to explain why that work matters.
  Describe what the applicant would learn to do with a teammate and a
  current skill they could apply to the same problem. Develop the story
  already in the letter. Describe plausible collaboration and verify any
  claims about how the team works. A small project can illustrate the
  connection when it fits the role.
  Keep it brief and grounded in what the team does. Describe how the team
  could check whether it helped. A relevant contribution to existing work
  is also enough.
- Use first person for honest curiosity, choices, and contributions. Let a
  specific ambition carry the enthusiasm. Keep the prose composed and
  conversational enough to sound like the applicant. Cut stock openings,
  repeated "I want" sentences, flattery, and lists of accomplishments that
  do not explain why the applicant is interested in this work.

Write about AI when it serves the story

- Show what changed in the applicant's practice if that change is relevant
  to the role. Explain how they use AI to research a problem, learn an
  unfamiliar tool, build a prototype, or verify its behavior. An
  independently built project can make that change tangible.
- Be clear about which tools the applicant used during development and
  which capabilities they built into the project. If a frontier model is
  relevant and the applicant asks to name it, use its precise public name
  when known. Describe their own use only when supported.
- Connect that experience to a specific AI workflow or question the
  applicant would like to learn about at this employer. Choose another
  interest for roles where it has a stronger connection.

Close with an interview invitation

- Before the sign-off, write a brief closing paragraph that conveys real
  interest in joining and explicitly asks for or welcomes an interview.
  Say what the applicant could discuss with the team when it adds something
  specific. Use direct language such as "I'd welcome an interview."
  Write the closing in the applicant's voice and vary it across letters.
  Keep it confident, warm, and free of a repeated project pitch.

Review and prepare the letter

- Read it as a hiring manager. Can you tell why the applicant would join
  this company and this team? If the reason would still read the same
  with another employer's name in its place, ground it in a more specific
  detail. Check that a reader can name the practice the applicant wants
  to develop, the problem they would work through with teammates to learn
  it, and the current skill they could bring to that work. Check that the
  final paragraph explicitly welcomes an interview.
- Keep the finished letter to one page at the standard PDF settings. Let
  the story determine the length and paragraph breaks. If it runs long, cut
  repetition and weaker details first. Use ordinary punctuation and keep the
  text free of em dashes.
- Use the employer name and a clean, recognizable job title in the PDF
  heading, with the verified location when available. Remove requisition
  numbers, job IDs, and internal posting or term codes. Keep meaningful
  title details such as the team, specialization, seniority, internship,
  or co-op designation. Include a season or year in ordinary words when
  it helps identify the role. Address a named hiring contact when the
  posting clearly identifies one. Otherwise use "Dear Hiring Manager,".
- For the portable PDF renderer, supply only the plain-text body, with a blank
  line between paragraphs. The renderer adds the header, date, greeting, and
  signature. For a text-only form, use the same prose with a greeting and
  sign-off if the field calls for a complete letter. Follow any employer
  requirement for applicant-authored text.
